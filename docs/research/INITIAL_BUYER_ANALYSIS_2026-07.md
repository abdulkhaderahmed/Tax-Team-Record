# Tax-Able initial buyer analysis

**Date:** 25 July 2026  
**Question:** Which stakeholder and company segment offers the strongest first market for an adviser-advice-to-tax-decision product?

## Decision

Tax-Able should sell to the **Head of Tax**.

- **Economic buyer and accountable owner:** Head of Tax or Group Head of Tax
- **Daily champion and principal user:** Tax Manager or Senior Tax Manager
- **Executive sponsor and funding approver:** CFO or Finance Director
- **Required collaborators:** Finance, Legal, Payroll, HR and Company Secretarial
- **Technology and security gatekeepers:** IT, information security and procurement
- **Source and possible referral channel:** External tax adviser

The first company segment should be:

- UK-headquartered private or AIM groups;
- approximately £200 million to £750 million turnover, with a lower boundary near £100 million where tax complexity is high;
- approximately 250 to 2,500 employees;
- a one-to-five-person in-house tax team;
- frequent use of external tax, legal or valuation advisers;
- several material transactions, employee-tax matters, enquiries or restructurings each year;
- existing compliance software or adviser filing support;
- advice implementation managed through email, SharePoint and spreadsheets.

Entity count should remain a secondary filter. A group with eight active entities and frequent transactions can have a stronger need than a static group with fifty entities.

## Meaning of “most lucrative”

The highest headline contract value is available among the UK’s largest groups. That segment also brings long procurement, security review, integration demands, incumbent enterprise software and a high burden of proof.

The useful measure for an early company is:

> expected annual contract value × probability of purchase × expansion potential ÷ sales and implementation cost

On this basis, the upper mid-market or lower end of large business offers a stronger initial return than a FTSE-scale buyer or a CFO-led company without an in-house tax function.

### Head of Tax

The Head of Tax owns the consequences Tax-Able is designed to control:

- advice reaches Tax too late;
- conditions in adviser reports are lost during implementation;
- business decisions wait for Finance, Legal or Payroll facts;
- earlier decisions are hard to reconstruct for HMRC, audit or a successor;
- tax technology, people and adviser budgets have to be justified;
- senior management expects a concise statement of risk and readiness.

This person can explain the loss, identify the first use case and sponsor adoption. Current research also shows that tax leadership increasingly owns technology strategy. In the Thomson Reuters 2026 survey, 49% of tax departments controlled their technology budget entirely, while 22% shared it with Finance and 13% with IT. Budget control is therefore common enough for direct Head of Tax sales, although Finance and IT need supporting material.

### Tax Manager

The Tax Manager experiences the highest daily friction. This person will judge whether the product saves time or creates administration. The role is an excellent design partner and internal champion.

Purchase authority is the constraint. A Tax Manager can introduce Tax-Able, populate it and prove its use. The Head of Tax normally has to approve the change and defend the spend.

The product should therefore optimise the working experience for the Tax Manager while making the value visible to the Head of Tax.

### CFO or Finance Director

The CFO controls budget and cares about transaction delay, unexpected tax exposure, HMRC risk and board assurance. Tax workflow is rarely a daily job for this person.

A CFO-led product would need to use general finance language and compete with broader governance, close-management and work-management products. The tax-specific record would become harder to explain. In companies without a tax leader, the frequency and willingness to pay are also lower.

The CFO should receive a one-page assurance view:

- material tax matters;
- decisions awaiting approval;
- decisions blocking transactions or filings;
- overdue requests by department;
- significant exceptions;
- evidence that agreed tax positions were implemented.

### External adviser

Advisers produce the source material and may refer the product to clients. Their economic incentives are mixed. A tool that reduces repeated explanation and missing-client-data work is useful; a tool positioned as replacing advice creates resistance and liability concerns.

Advisers should be a source and distribution channel after the in-house workflow has been proven. They should not define the initial product.

## Evidence for the buyer choice

### There is a meaningful company population, but employee count overstates the market

The UK had 38,435 medium-sized private businesses with 50 to 249 employees and 8,335 large private businesses with at least 250 employees at the start of 2025. These figures provide an upper boundary. Many of these businesses have no dedicated tax function.

HMRC separately reports around 2,000 businesses in its Large Business population. These companies have clear governance pressure, although many will have the procurement and integration burden that an early product should avoid.

Sources:

- [UK business population estimates 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/business-population-estimates-2025/business-population-estimates-for-the-uk-and-regions-2025-statistical-release)
- [HMRC tax by customer group, 2025 to 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/hmrc-annual-report-and-accounts-2025-to-2026-technical-notes/tax-by-different-customer-groups-2025-to-2026--2)

### Governance pressure becomes visible around £200 million

UK groups generally enter the published tax strategy and Senior Accounting Officer tests when prior-year turnover exceeds £200 million or the balance sheet exceeds £2 billion. These thresholds do not define every buyer. They provide a useful signal that tax governance has a named executive owner and a documented process requirement.

Sources:

- [HMRC: publish your tax strategy](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/large-businesses-publish-your-tax-strategy)
- [HMRC Senior Accounting Officer qualifying-company conditions](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/senior-accounting-officers-guidance/saog11210)

### Administrative burden and cross-functional delay are buyer problems

HMRC’s 2025 Large Business survey covered 558 large businesses and 30 follow-up interviews. Thirty-seven per cent disagreed that the administrative burden of tax compliance was reasonable, the highest disagreement in the survey series.

Tolley surveyed 93 in-house tax professionals in England and Wales during 2025:

- 41% expected tax technology budgets to rise;
- 32% described communication with other departments as slow or very slow;
- 57% said embedding new technology was the slowest-moving area;
- respondents identified keeping up with tax updates and budget constraints as leading concerns.

Sources:

- [HMRC Large Business Customer Survey 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-large-business-customer-survey-2025/the-large-business-customer-survey-2025-executive-summary)
- [Tolley In-house Tax Technology Report 2025](https://www.lexisnexis.co.uk/tolley/tax/insights/in-house-tax-technology-report-2025/index.html)

### The buying case must account for implementation capacity

The Thomson Reuters 2026 Corporate Tax Technology Report surveyed 170 tax leaders, with a strong North American bias. Only 27% said they could obtain the budget and resources they needed when needed; 34% said budget and implementation resources were both constrained.

This makes a light first deployment commercially important. Tax-Able should accept one adviser document and create a useful matter before requesting entity, registration or source-system setup.

Source:

- [Thomson Reuters 2026 Corporate Tax Technology Report](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/03/2026-Corporate-Tax-Technology-Report.pdf)

### HMRC already values systems, controls and evidence

HMRC’s Business Risk Review guidance asks whether a large business has adequate systems, processes and tax resources. It treats robust systems and a tax risk and controls matrix as indicators of a sustainable compliance process.

Tax-Able can support that operating evidence. It should avoid claiming that use of the product proves a legal treatment or guarantees a favourable risk rating.

Source:

- [HMRC Business Risk Review: systems and delivery](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/tax-compliance-risk-management/tcrm3342)

The scoring is a product hypothesis. Interviews and paid trials must replace it with observed buying behaviour.

## Revenue hypothesis

The following figures are price tests rather than market facts:

- paid proof period: £5,000 to £10,000 for one advice archetype and two live matters;
- initial annual contract: £25,000 to £45,000 for a small tax team;
- expanded contract: £45,000 to £75,000 where several tax regimes, approvers and business units use the system.

At this price, the buyer needs to see at least one of:

- a material decision or condition that would otherwise have been missed;
- a measurable reduction in adviser-report triage and follow-up;
- faster completion of Finance, Legal or Payroll requests;
- a reusable decision and evidence record for HMRC or audit;
- avoided rework when facts, staff or advisers change.

The product should not be priced as a task manager. The sales case is control over material tax decisions and their implementation.

## Reachable market test using FAME

The public data cannot identify how many groups employ a Head of Tax. FAME can produce a better estimate if employer policy and the database licence permit aggregate market research.

Suggested first query:

1. Active UK-headquartered companies or groups.
2. Latest turnover between £100 million and £750 million.
3. At least 250 employees, or fewer employees where turnover exceeds £200 million.
4. Exclude banks, insurers, public bodies and dormant holding companies.
5. Add indicators for subsidiaries, recent acquisitions and international operations.
6. Deduplicate to the ultimate UK parent.
7. Review a sample of 100 companies for a Head of Tax, Tax Director or Group Tax Manager using public professional profiles and company websites.

Record aggregate counts and percentages. Do not place licensed FAME rows, personal profile data or employer-confidential information in this repository.

This exercise should answer:

- the proportion with a named tax leader;
- the sectors with the highest tax-leader incidence;
- the proportion with recent acquisition or restructuring activity;
- the likely number of reachable UK groups;
- whether £100 million or £200 million is the better lower boundary.

## Sales motion

1. **Find the Head of Tax** through tax-leader networks, adviser introductions and targeted company research.
2. **Qualify operating pain** by asking for the last three material adviser reports and how each recommendation was implemented.
3. **Demo one matter** created from an adviser PDF. Avoid a broad product tour.
4. **Include the Tax Manager** to test whether review and follow-up are faster.
5. **Give the CFO a short assurance case** covering decision delay, risk, ownership and evidence.
6. **Bring IT and security in early** with a narrow data flow and deployment statement.
7. **Run a paid proof period** against the incumbent email, SharePoint and spreadsheet process.

## Product and demo consequences

The fictional workspace should represent:

- Northstar Group, a UK-headquartered private group;
- approximately £430 million revenue and 1,850 employees;
- fourteen active legal entities;
- a three-person in-house tax team;
- regular external adviser reports;
- existing compliance and filing tools;
- advice implementation managed through email, documents and spreadsheets before Tax-Able.

The first demo action should be **upload adviser advice**.

The demo should show:

1. the matter brief created with minimal setup;
2. conditions and caveats preserved from the source pages;
3. Tax Manager review of the proposed controls;
4. Finance, Legal or Payroll requests;
5. Head of Tax decision and approval;
6. evidence and decision history;
7. a concise CFO assurance view.

## Falsification tests

Interview fifteen Heads of Tax and ten Tax Managers matching the target segment.

The thesis weakens if:

- fewer than one-third receive material adviser documents at least monthly;
- fewer than one-third rely on email or spreadsheets to track implementation;
- tax conditions are rarely lost after advice is delivered;
- Heads of Tax will not pay for this outside an existing compliance suite;
- the Tax Manager review takes as long as reading the source report directly;
- CFOs see no value in the resulting assurance record;
- security review makes a document-first proof period impractical.

The thesis strengthens when at least three companies agree to a paid proof period using live, redacted or synthetic versions of their own adviser documents.
